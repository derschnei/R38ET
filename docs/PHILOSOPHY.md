# Philosophy — The Foundations of R38ET

This document is the intellectual lineage of R38ET, not its programme. The Manifesto states what we want. This document states where the wanting comes from: which books, which experiments, which failures, which technical decisions, and which parts of the record fail to support the claim being made.

It is written to be inspected. Every section names what R38ET takes, and — where the evidence is thin — says so. Where an argument is ours and not the source's, that is marked. Where an example is used as a warning rather than a model, that is marked too. The document should be arguable at the level of individual paragraphs, and it should be correctable without changing the shape of the whole. A lineage that cannot be revised is a catechism, and this is not one.

---

## 1. The History of Commons

Council systems failed at coordination cost, not at principle. That is the opening argument, and it is meant literally: the historical record does not show that self-government by assemblies produces worse decisions than its alternatives, nor that it is morally incoherent. It shows that assemblies repeatedly lost to centralised states in the one competition that decided the outcome — the ability to reach a binding decision across distance and to act on it before the situation changed.

What could not cross distance could not be governed. A federation whose members are three days apart by horse is not a federation; it is a set of local governments that occasionally agree. Recallable delegates, imperative mandates, confederal congresses — the machinery was invented early and worked, at the scale at which messages travelled in days. Above that scale, the state with its telegraph, its standing administration and its fiscal apparatus was simply faster.

The question this chapter poses is therefore not historical but structural: **is coordination cost now a design parameter rather than a law of nature?** If the cost of transmitting a proposal, aggregating a position, verifying a vote and publishing a decision has fallen by orders of magnitude, then the constraint that selected for the nation-state has loosened. That does not by itself make council government work. It removes one specific reason why it did not work before. Whether that is enough is the open question of the whole document, and it is answered by argument, not by precedent — because there is no precedent.

### 1.1 Theoretical Works

**Elinor Ostrom — *Governing the Commons* (1990).** Ostrom's contribution is not the observation that commons can be managed; it is the specification of how. Her design principles — clearly defined boundaries, rules congruent with local conditions, collective choice arrangements, monitoring by accountable parties, graduated sanctions, cheap conflict resolution, recognition of the right to self-organise, and *nested enterprises* for larger systems — describe an institutional grammar that survives scrutiny because it was derived from cases that worked and cases that failed.

R38ET takes two things. First, the nested-enterprises principle is the structural ancestor of the eight-level council system: Ostrom's finding is that large commons systems are not governed by one rule set but by a nested hierarchy of rule sets, each fitted to its own scale, each with its own monitoring and conflict resolution. Our cascade is that observation given a technical substrate. Second, and more importantly, her insistence that rules be made by those subject to them is the direct justification for a Git-native Norm Library: if the people bound by a norm must have made it, then the record of who wrote what, when, and with which mandate is not administrative metadata — it is the legitimacy of the norm itself. A version-controlled, signed, forkable record of norms is the closest thing to Ostrom's principle that a large system can actually implement.

**Peter Kropotkin — *Mutual Aid* (1902).** Kropotkin's argument is empirical, not moral: he assembles evidence of cooperation in animal populations and in human institutions — guilds, communes, mutual aid societies — and concludes that cooperation is an observed practice of successful populations rather than a virtue to be exhorted. That distinction matters for us, because a movement that argues from how people *ought* to behave has to explain why they do not; a movement that argues from how people *do* behave under the right conditions has only to build the conditions.

The limit: Kropotkin's evidence is abundant but anecdotal, and he tends to treat cooperative institutions as self-evidently superior rather than as solutions to specific problems. R38ET keeps the naturalism and drops the teleology. Cooperation happens; it is not guaranteed to win.

**Murray Bookchin — social ecology and libertarian municipalism.** Bookchin's two contributions are the claim that ecological problems are rooted in social hierarchy, and the programmatic proposal that free municipalities, linked in confederation, are the appropriate unit of democratic ecological governance. The first is a claim about causes, the second about scale.

R38ET takes the confederal structure and rejects the geography. Bookchin's municipality is a place; his confederation is a league of places. Our councils are also not arbitrary — they have territorial extent — but the mandate is what defines them, not the boundary: a council exists to hold a specific competence, delegated from below and revocable. The consequence is that scale is a question of mandate, not of geography. A competence can be delegated upward as far as it must travel and no further; the map is an output of that decision, not its premise. This also disposes of the standard objection to municipalism — that the free municipality is a romantic unit which the modern economy long ago rendered irrelevant — because we are not claiming the municipality as the natural unit. We are claiming that the unit is chosen by the delegation, which may be a neighbourhood, a watershed, a city, or a continent.

**Hannah Arendt — *On Revolution* (1963).** Arendt's discussion of the council tradition — the Paris Commune, the Russian soviets, the Hungarian workers' councils of 1956 — is the clearest statement of what councils are *for*. They are spaces of appearance: places where people act in public, constitute themselves as political beings, and experience freedom as participation rather than as protection. Her complaint against the party and the state is not that they decide badly but that they make action unnecessary, and thereby make citizens into administered subjects.

We take the insight and refuse the fatalism. Arendt treats the disappearance of councils as a recurring tragedy rooted in the nature of political action — the public realm is fragile, and the administrative apparatus always absorbs it. R38ET's wager is that fragility has a substantial technical component: the public realm kept collapsing because it had no durable record, no way to persist between meetings, no memory independent of the people in the room. A council whose decisions, debates and mandates survive in an immutable, public, forkable ledger has a defence against absorption that the 1871 Commune did not. Whether that is sufficient to defeat Arendt's pessimism is not settled; we simply decline to treat the pessimism as a law and treat it instead as a design requirement.

**Cornelius Castoriadis — *The Imaginary Institution of Society* (1975).** Castoriadis argues that societies are instituted by imaginary significations — shared meanings that are neither natural facts nor logical deductions, and which a society typically does not recognise as its own creation. Autonomy, in his sense, is the capacity of a society to put its own institutions and significations into question, rather than to live them as given.

This is the philosophical justification for a Norm Library. If autonomy means the capacity to question one's own rules, then the rules must be available as objects — inspectable, dated, attributed, with their justifications attached and their alternatives preserved. A rule known only as custom cannot be questioned; a rule that exists as a signed commit with a diff and an argument can be. The repository is not a filing cabinet for decisions; it is the material precondition of collective autonomy.

Castoriadis also supplies the warning that shapes the design. He distinguishes the *institution of autonomy* from autonomous institutions, and warns that an institution founded on explicit and permanent questioning tends to calcify into the ritual of questioning — the form survives, the substance becomes a procedure performed for its own sake. A Norm Library that is technically impeccable and politically inert is exactly this failure. R38ET therefore treats the repository as necessary but insufficient, and treats participation at the low levels — not the quality of the diffs — as the actual test of whether autonomy is alive.

**Jürgen Habermas — deliberative legitimacy.** Habermas's account of legitimacy holds that a norm is valid when it could be accepted by all affected parties in a free and unconstrained deliberation. Whatever one makes of the counterfactual, it converts into concrete conditions: affected parties must have access, unequal power must not determine the outcome, arguments must be answerable, and the agenda must not be controlled by whoever benefits from it.

R38ET treats these as engineering requirements rather than ideals, and assigns each to a component:

- **The mesh carries the speech.** Reticulum/LXMF transport means deliberation does not require permission from a platform, an ISP or a state. Access is a matter of radio range and keys, not of terms of service.
- **The Norm Library carries the information.** Arguments, positions and justifications are recorded where they can be cited, checked and revisited — not lost in a chat scroll.
- **Paritarian dual leadership and the imperative mandate keep the agenda from capture.** Two co-chairs with equal authority and opposed backgrounds make unilateral agenda-setting structurally difficult; the imperative mandate, which binds a delegate to the position of those who sent them and permits recall at any time, means the delegate cannot convert access into a private power base.
- **Weighted voting by population** keeps representation proportional, so that deliberation is not a conversation among the numerically irrelevant.

The honest qualification: Habermas's conditions are counterfactual, and our implementations are approximations with known gaps. Captured agendas are made harder, not impossible. Access to radio is not universal. The claim is that the conditions are addressed explicitly and can be audited, not that they are met.

### 1.2 Communal Examples

Theoretical lineage tells what was thought. This section tells what was attempted, including the attempts that ended badly, because a lineage that cites only successes is a selection effect.

**The Diggers (1649).** Gerrard Winstanley's community on St George's Hill occupied and cultivated common land at the exact moment when enclosure was converting common land into private property across England. The Diggers contested enclosure not by petition but by practice, and their argument was theological and economic at once: the earth is a common treasury, and the poor have a right to cultivate what has been taken.

They were dispersed within a year by local landowners and the army, and their settlement never became anything but a demonstration. The Diggers are the origin point of the argument rather than a precedent for the institution: they show that the enclosure of the commons was contested at the moment it happened, and they show how little a moral claim matters against a jurisdictional one. They had right on their side and no standing. The lesson R38ET draws is uncomfortable for a movement that likes the Diggers: a moral test is not a jurisdictional one, and a common without a recognised authority to defend it is an invitation.

**The Paris Commune (1871).** Seventy-two days. The Commune's institutional innovations are precisely the ones our model depends on: delegates to the Commune council were elected by ward assemblies, bound by imperative mandates, and subject to immediate recall; the council was a working body rather than a parliament, with executive commissions answerable to it. This is the demonstration — the only clean one in the modern record — that recallable delegates with imperative mandates function at a scale well above the face-to-face assembly. They worked, for ten weeks, in a besieged city.

Two failures. The first was coordination with the provinces: the Commune had no means of building a confederal structure quickly, and the national government had railways and the telegraph. The second, and more serious for us, is that the Commune never resolved the question of legitimate force. It inherited an army, a National Guard loyal to it, and no settled doctrine about who may use coercion and under what authority. That question ended the Commune in a week of street fighting, and R38ET does not have a better answer — it has the same open question, displaced into a constitution rather than a barricade.

**The council movements of 1918–19.** The German Räte, the Bavarian council republic, the Hungarian soviets, the workers' councils of the immediate post-war period. This is the central case for the thesis of this chapter, because these movements were not marginal: they had mass support, articulated programmes, and in several places actual state power for a period of weeks or months.

They lost a race against coordination cost, not an argument about legitimacy. Councils in Berlin, Munich, Budapest and Vienna could not deliberate continuously with one another; telegraphy carried statements but not deliberation, and every decision required a plenary that could only exist in one room. Meanwhile the older apparatus — ministries, general staffs, party organisations with permanent staff — could act as a single body across the same territory. The failure was logistical. R38ET's claim is that the specific bottleneck that decided 1919 is now cheap. That claim is testable in principle and is the reason this project is not merely a restatement of 1919 with better intentions.

**Rojava.** The closest existing relative, and the one whose institutions most resemble ours. The Democratic Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria organises through commune assemblies at the neighbourhood and village level, larger people's councils above them, and confederal coordination across cantons. Co-chairmanship with mandatory parity between a woman and a man at every level of every council is not advisory but constitutive; the system descends from Abdullah Öcalan's democratic confederalism and from a feminist critique of the state form as such.

Two unresolved problems, stated plainly. First, cohesion under siege: Rojava's political unity has been produced to a significant degree by external military threat. It is not known how these institutions behave in the absence of an enemy, and a system that requires permanent emergency to hold together is not a model for a peaceful continent. Second, the relation between confederal norms and local deviation is not settled in Rojava's practice. There is a real tension between the confederation's founding commitments — parity, ecology, direct democracy — and the autonomy of the commune, and it is managed politically rather than resolved institutionally. R38ET inherits this problem and attempts to answer it with the Lex specialis mechanism and a defined class of non-delegable norms. Whether our answer is better than their management is not established.

**Ecovillages (GEN Europe and the wider network).** Ecovillages demonstrate that self-governing communities of a few dozen to a few hundred people can manage shared resources, resolve conflicts and sustain commitment over decades. As evidence that the social problem is soluble at small scale, they are strong.

Their bias must be named: they are voluntary. Membership is self-selected, exit is available, and the community's cohesion is maintained substantially by the fact that people who do not fit leave. Self-selection substitutes for institutional machinery — in a community where everyone chose to be there, you need less procedure to produce compliance. A town of two hundred thousand people who did not choose each other and cannot leave is a different problem, and ecovillages are not evidence about that problem. R38ET's model must work for those who did not opt in, and no ecovillage proves that it can.

**Transition Towns.** The Transition movement, from Totnes onward, is the most instructive case in this list for what an organisation can and cannot do. Its central technique is what might be called the cheerful disclaimer: it explicitly does not tell communities what to do, does not prescribe outcomes, does not take positions on contested policies — it offers a process, a set of tools and a network, and lets each initiative find its own way. That disclaimer is not modesty; it is an organisational technique that allowed a framework to spread across thousands of initiatives in dozens of countries without anyone having to agree on a programme.

And that is precisely its ceiling. Transition initiatives advise, inspire and connect. They do not allocate resources, adjudicate disputes with binding force, or oblige anyone to comply. There is no mechanism by which a Transition initiative can require anything of a resident of its town. This is not a criticism of the movement — it chose its scope — but it means Transition Towns are evidence about propagation and motivation, not about governance.

**DEAL networks.** The Doughnut Economics Action Group network shows how a framework propagates without permission from its authors. Cities and municipalities adopt the framework, adapt it, and are recognised as part of a network without being licensed by anyone. This is a genuinely interesting model of diffusion: the framework's authors have no authority over the adopters, and the network's coherence comes from shared use rather than from hierarchy.

But a network of adopters is not a jurisdiction. A city that declares itself doughnut-shaped has made a commitment, not created an obligation. Nothing in the DEAL network binds a subsequent city government, and nothing prevents an adopter from using the vocabulary while doing something else. R38ET borrows the diffusion model and must supply what it lacks: bindingness.

**The honest summary.** Ecovillages and Transition Towns are voluntary communities, not territorial jurisdictions. Historical communes had jurisdiction and lacked coordination capacity: they could bind people, and could not reach them. Contemporary networks have coordination capacity and lack jurisdiction: they can reach people, and cannot bind them. R38ET claims both can now be had at once, in the same structure. That claim is unproven, and no item in the record above demonstrates it. The strongest available support is indirect: each of the two halves exists in working form, and the constraint that kept them apart — coordination cost — has demonstrably fallen. That is an argument, not a proof, and the document says so deliberately.

### 1.3 Diagnoses

**Garrett Hardin — "The Tragedy of the Commons" (1968).** Hardin's parable describes a pasture open to all, in which each herder's rational move is to add one more animal, with the result that the pasture is destroyed. The argument became the most cited justification for privatisation and state regulation of common resources.

Its central error is terminological and it is decisive: Hardin describes an *open-access* resource, not a commons. A commons is a resource governed by an institution; an unmanaged pasture is a resource governed by nobody, and Hardin's herders behave as they do precisely because there is no institution. Ostrom's empirical work is the refutation: communities across centuries and continents have managed shared resources sustainably where boundaries, rules, monitoring and sanctions existed. The tragedy occurs in the absence of an institution.

Hardin remains useful in exactly that role. He is an accurate description of what happens when there is no institution — when a resource is nominally common but actually unowned, unmonitored and ungoverned. R38ET cites him as the description of the failure mode we are built to prevent, not as an argument we must answer.

**John Maynard Keynes — the diagnosis without the therapy.** Keynes identified the central economic problem of his century correctly: coordination failure is structural, not moral. Economies can settle into equilibria in which everyone is worse off, and no amount of individual virtue corrects this, because the problem is in the relations between decisions rather than in the deciders. The General Theory is a systematic account of how an aggregate can behave badly while every component behaves rationally.

R38ET borrows the diagnosis and declines the therapy. Keynes's remedy — demand management, countercyclical spending, the deliberate use of the state's fiscal power to hold the system near full employment — is bound to an entity with fiscal sovereignty. It requires a body that can tax, borrow in its own currency, and spend against the cycle. That unit no longer matches the size of the problems: the ecological ceiling is global, financial flows cross borders faster than any fiscal authority can respond, and the institutions Keynes designed operate inside containers that leak. This is not a failure of Keynes's intelligence; the container he worked with was the one that existed.

It is also worth stating what Keynes sought and did not seek. He wanted a fairer order inside capitalism, and he said so: his ambition was to preserve the system while removing its worst distributional and stability failures. He did not attempt to transcend it. R38ET is not a Keynesian project with a global vocabulary; it takes from Keynes the claim that coordination failure is real and structural — which is also our claim about 1919 — and then locates the coordinating institution somewhere other than the sovereign state.

**The coordination-cost argument, stated as a diagnosis.** Logistical capacity, not ideology, explains the outcome of 1919 and the survival of the nation-state as the default political unit. Before the telegraph and the railway, the largest territory that could be governed by continuous deliberation was small. With them, the nation-state became the smallest unit that could deliberate continuously across its territory — and therefore the natural unit of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. It was not theoretically correct; it was the smallest thing that fit the communication technology of the time.

If that is the diagnosis, then the nation-state's dominance is contingent on a technology that has changed. The argument is empirical and falsifiable in structure: either the cost of continuous, verifiable, many-to-many deliberation across distance has fallen below the threshold that made the state necessary, or it has not. R38ET asserts it has. The mesh layer in section 2.3 is the concrete form of that assertion, and the eight-level cascade is what one does with the capacity once it exists. A reader who rejects this diagnosis will find little else in the document persuasive, which is why it is placed early and stated as an assertion rather than a conclusion.

## 2. Technical Lineage

Ideas do not become institutions by being correct. The components below are the ones R38ET borrows from, and in each case the borrowing is specific: a mechanism, a guarantee, a limit.

### 2.1 Aggregation

**RadicalxChange.** The RadicalxChange circle around Glen Weyl produced two ideas of direct use. Quadratic mechanisms — quadratic voting, quadratic funding — price intensity of preference rather than counting heads, so that a minority with strong preferences is not automatically defeated by an indifferent majority, while collusion becomes expensive. And sortition, treated not as a fallback for when elections are impossible but as a legitimate selection method in its own right: random selection produces bodies that are descriptively representative, immune to campaign finance, and free of the incentive to compete for power.

R38ET adopts both as available mechanisms rather than as doctrine. Sortition in particular is used where a body must be assembled without an election becoming a power contest — juries, review panels, agenda-setting bodies at low levels.

**pol.is.** pol.is solves a problem that voting does not: it clusters participants by opinion and surfaces the statements that are agreed across clusters, while preserving the statements that define a minority position instead of collapsing everything into a majority percentage. The output is not a winner but a map — here is what most groups can accept, here is what one group holds and the others do not. In practice, this has allowed large-scale consultations to produce positions that a referendum would have destroyed.

R38ET uses the pattern rather than the product: aggregation should reveal the structure of disagreement, and any council record that reports only the count has discarded information the deliberation produced.

**The design consequence.** Aggregation technique and representation are two different problems, and conflating them is a common error. Weighted voting by population answers *who is represented and in what proportion*; quadratic or clustered aggregation answers *how preferences are combined without destroying minorities*. R38ET keeps both, separately: **weighted voting by population plus an imperative direct mandate across all levels.** The weighting keeps the council from becoming a conversation among the unrepresentative; the imperative direct mandate keeps the delegate from becoming a separate interest; the aggregation layer decides how the weighted positions combine. The specification of which mechanism applies at which level belongs in the architecture document, not here — but the philosophical commitment is that no level aggregates in a way that conceals the minority view, because the record is meant to be readable afterwards by people who were not present.

### 2.2 Formal Methods

**Catala as NormLinter.** Catala is a domain-specific language for translating legal and administrative text into code, designed so that the source text and the executable logic remain traceable to one another. Its purpose is not to replace the lawyer or the legislator but to expose ambiguity: a rule that cannot be expressed without arbitrary choices is a rule with unspecified cases.

R38ET uses it as a linter for norms. A norm in the library can be submitted to formal checking for internal consistency, for conflicts with norms at other levels, and for undefined cases. The value is not enforcement but legibility: a norm whose consequences are computable can be argued about precisely, and a council that adopts it can see what it is adopting.

**Deontic logic** supplies the vocabulary for that checking — obligation, permission, prohibition, and their interactions. The reason it matters is that most conflicts between levels are not contradictions of fact but collisions between a permission at one level and a prohibition at another. Formalising the modality is what makes the Lex specialis mechanism checkable at all, rather than a matter of opinion about whether two rules "really" conflict.

**The honest limit.** Formal consistency is not material compatibility. A rule can be formally consistent with a norm above it and substantively an evasion of it: drafted to satisfy the letter, computed to be valid, and understood by everyone affected as a way around the constraint. No linter detects this, because it is not a formal property — it is a judgment about purpose. This is why the burden of proof in R38ET runs the other way: consistency is checked locally, with Catala as a tool, and challenged upward only by an actual complaint. The rule is permitted until successfully contested, not forbidden until proven compliant. That is a real transfer of power, and it is also an invitation to evasion. We accept the trade and name it rather than resolving it.

### 2.3 Mesh and Sovereignty

**Reticulum and LXMF.** Reticulum is a networking stack built for operation without central infrastructure: it runs over LoRa radio, packet radio, serial links, TCP, anything that moves bits, and it treats all of them as interchangeable. Addresses are cryptographic, so identity and routing are the same thing and there is no registry to capture. LXMF is the messaging layer on top, carrying store-and-forward messages across intermittent links.

The point is not that the mesh is faster or cheaper. The point is that the transport does not require permission. A system whose deliberation depends on a platform can be switched off by that platform; a system whose deliberation runs over encrypted packets between devices in radio range cannot be switched off without switching off the radios. This is the technical content of the claim that coordination cost is now a design parameter: it is not that everyone has a smartphone, it is that the network does not need an owner.

**SAS commit-reveal as Sybil resistance.** Any system in which one person's weight counts must answer the question of how many persons there are, and the usual answer is an identity authority — a state, a platform, a registry. R38ET refuses that answer, because an identity authority is a single point of capture and a permanent substrate of surveillance.

Instead: short authentication strings, verified in person or over an existing trusted channel, combined with commit-reveal so that enrolment cannot be front-run or harvested. Two people who meet confirm each other's keys by comparing a short code; the commitment is published before the reveal, so no one can adjust their contribution after seeing others'. Sybil resistance then comes from the social graph — the cost of manufacturing an identity is the cost of finding a human who will vouch for it — rather than from a database. This is weaker than a state registry, and it is honest about that weakness: it scales with the density and integrity of the human network, which is exactly the thing a movement has or does not have.

**Git as the DAG ledger.** Decisions, norms, attestations and mandates are signed commits in a hash-linked, append-only structure. It provides content addressing, tamper evidence, complete provenance, and cheap forking. Its politics are also appropriate: no commit is authoritative because of who holds the server; it is authoritative because of the signatures on it and the history that leads to it.

**The architectural decision: Git is the record layer, RRC is the presence layer.** A chat transport is the wrong place for deliberation. A council debating a norm needs the proposal, the amendments, the reasoning, the outcome and the minority opinion together, in order and addressable weeks later; it needs contributions that can be cited, answered and carried into a vote; and it needs the deliberation to sit inside the same trust model as the result, or the record has a gap between decision and justification that can never be closed afterwards. Chat is none of these things — and that is not a UI problem, it is what the medium is.

So: consultative procedure runs as a branch, a proposal is a commit, an amendment is a commit on top of it, a decision is a signed merge. The record of how a decision was made is then in the same ledger as the decision. For the lower levels, where not everyone operates Git, a lightweight append-only discussion object carries the deliberation — signed entries referencing the proposal, exported to Git when the deliberation closes — so that the bridge between a phone keyboard and the ledger exists without a second system of record.

RRC remains, in the role it is actually suited to: presence. One channel per board, where people find each other, arrange a SAS handshake, announce a session, ask a question that does not need to become a document. Being ephemeral there is not a defect but the function. The defect would be attempting to make it the forum.

## 3. Civil Society Precedents

The standing objection to any transnational assembly is that it cannot exist without a world state: without sovereignty there is no bindingness, and without bindingness there is only talk. The organisations below are the empirical answer to that objection. They are not sources of R38ET's values — the values are argued in section 1 — and they are not models of internal governance. They are evidence that transnational structures function without sovereignty.

- **Amnesty International** (1961) built a global membership organisation around a single, non-negotiable object: the individual prisoner. Its authority derives from documentation rather than jurisdiction, and it has been effective enough that governments respond to its reports as though they were binding.
- **Greenpeace** demonstrated that a transnational organisation can act directly and physically across borders, funding itself from individual donors and refusing institutional money, and can set the terms of public debate in countries where it holds no legal standing.
- **Médecins Sans Frontières** established that the delivery of a universal good can be organised across borders by a private association, on the principle of neutrality and independent assessment, in places where no state authority is functioning.
- **terre des hommes** shows the same pattern at the scale of a children's rights organisation: national sections, a shared charter, programme work delivered across jurisdictions, and no sovereign power anywhere in the structure.
- **ICAN** is the strongest single case. The International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons coordinated a coalition of civil society organisations into a diplomatic process that produced the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons — a binding legal instrument adopted in 2017 and in force since 2021, negotiated without the participation of the nuclear-armed states and without any world state to enact it. Whatever one thinks of the treaty's enforcement prospects, the instrument exists, and it was produced by transnational civil society.
- **Transparency International** made corruption measurable. Its Corruption Perceptions Index converted a phenomenon that had been discussed as a moral failing of particular nations into an ordinal, comparable, annually published quantity — and in doing so created a metric that governments compete on. Measurement as a form of power without jurisdiction.
- **FSFE and FSF** maintained a principle rather than an institution: software freedom, stated in 1985 as four freedoms and defended continuously since, including through a licence — the GPL — that enforces itself through copyright rather than through an authority. This is the closest analogue in the list to what R38ET is attempting with a Norm Library: a rule set that binds by construction and propagates by adoption.
- **Oxfam** shows a large international confederation of national affiliates operating under shared standards without a sovereign centre, and, in the case of the Doughnut, funding the production of a framework that others then adopted.
- **GEN Europe and the Transition Network** are included for completeness, and with the caveat already entered in section 1.2: they are networks of voluntary communities, and their coherence comes from shared practice, not from obligation.

The pattern across all of them: a transnational body can accumulate authority, produce binding instruments, set measurements and maintain principles, without holding sovereignty and without a world state behind it. What they do not demonstrate is *democratic* authority — these organisations are not elected by the people they affect, and their accountability is to members and funders. That is the gap R38ET claims to fill, and the precedents establish only that the structural possibility is real.

## 4. Ecological Framing

The framework requires two boundaries: a floor below which no one should fall, and a ceiling above which the systems that support life are destabilised. Both are now supplied by bodies of work that are quantitative, published and contested in ways that can be inspected.

**The Doughnut.** Kate Raworth's formulation, first published as an Oxfam discussion paper in 2012 and developed in *Doughnut Economics* (2017), combines a social foundation — the goods without which a life cannot be lived decently: food, water, health, education, energy, income, political voice — with an ecological ceiling. Between them lies the safe and just space for humanity. Its structure is precisely the double structure the model needs: obligations below as well as limits above, so that the framework cannot be satisfied by austerity. The Doughnut has since been adopted by cities and governments as an operating framework, which makes it one of the few conceptual proposals of the last decade to have crossed into practice.

**Planetary boundaries.** Rockström and colleagues (2009) identified nine Earth-system processes that regulate the stability of the planet and quantified, for each, a safe operating space — climate change, biosphere integrity, biogeochemical flows, ocean acidification, freshwater use, land-system change, stratospheric ozone, atmospheric aerosols, novel entities. Subsequent assessments have found several boundaries already transgressed; the 2023 assessment placed six of the nine beyond their safe limits. This is the source of the ceiling, and it is scientific rather than negotiated in the sense that the processes and their control variables are empirical.

**The 2023 safe-and-just boundaries.** Rockström and colleagues' 2023 work quantifies boundaries that are simultaneously biophysically safe and distributionally just — limits defined not only by what the Earth system can tolerate but by what allocation between people and between generations avoids exposing anyone to significant harm. This is, in its own vocabulary, exactly the framework's formulation: the ceiling is not merely physical but justified, and the justification is distributive. The work that might have been left open is largely done, and R38ET relies on it.

**The distinction that the chapter turns on.** The science settles the ceiling. It does not settle the allocation. Moving from a global boundary to a council's share requires converting a biophysical control variable into a global budget and then dividing that budget — and the division is not a physical question. It is a distributive question, and the literature names it as such: questions of fair shares, historical responsibility, capability and need. No expert body can settle it without inheriting the legitimacy question, because there is no scientific answer to "who gets how much" that does not smuggle in a normative premise.

R38ET therefore separates the two. **The ceiling is scientific input; the allocation is a council decision under the imperative mandate, contestable at every level.** Concretely: the boundaries enter the system as a downward-flowing constraint, expressed in a form that can be checked (section 2.2); the procedure by which the global budget is downscaled is defined at the highest level but is not executed there; and every allocation decision is a decision of a council whose members are bound by the mandate of those they represent and removable by them. A council that disputes its allocation disputes it politically, with reasons, in a record — not by appealing to a body of experts whose authority would then have to be either obeyed or destroyed.

This is also the defence against the charge of eco-technocracy, and the defence must be structural rather than rhetorical. An environmental framework imposed by technical authority is a green expert government, whatever its vocabulary. The claim here is that the two components are kept apart by design: the science contributes limits and their justification; the councils contribute allocation and bear the political cost of it. If the allocation were handed down, the charge would be correct. If the limit were open to local veto, the ceiling would be decorative. The architecture exists to hold that line, and where it fails — where an allocation decision is effectively taken by whoever computes the budget — that is a defect to be reported, not a nuance to be explained away.

A final admission. The imperative mandate guarantees that allocation decisions are *contestable*, not that they are *wise*, and not that they will be made in time. Ecological constraints operate on schedules that do not respect deliberation. The framework's answer — that a contested, mandated, recordable decision is more likely to be implemented than an imposed one — is a claim about legitimacy translating into compliance, and it is not proven. It is, however, the only claim available to anyone who refuses both expert rule and inaction.
