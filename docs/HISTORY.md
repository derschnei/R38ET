# History — A Register of Commons and Councils

Entries are ordered by the date they begin, not by the date they ended, and the dates are approximate wherever the sources are. Each entry answers three questions: what was attempted, how it was organised, and why it ended or who destroyed it. The register records attempts, not ideas — the ideas belong in PHILOSOPHY.md, and nothing appears here that somebody actually did not try to build.

**c. 3000 BCE — Mesopotamian irrigation and canal commons (Sumer, between Tigris and Euphrates).** The oldest works in the register are water works: canals, levees and reservoirs maintained jointly by the settlements that drew on them, with water shares and maintenance duties entered in temple and palace accounts, and disputes over silting and the theft of water brought before the temple assembly. The organisation ran through the temple household, which held the stores and kept the record, in cities whose land was largely temple land; the common was administered rather than held. It ended twice over. The canals silted and the irrigated fields salted up — the accounts show declining yields for generations before any conquest — and the palace absorbed the temple's administration, so that a water share became a levy and a maintenance duty a corvée. What survived was the record of an obligation, not a body of users who held it.

**594 BCE — Solon's seisachtheia and the abolition of debt bondage (Athens).** Called to the archonship with extraordinary powers in a debt crisis, Solon cancelled the debts secured on land and on person (the seisachtheia, the "shaking off of burdens"), abolished debt bondage and brought back citizens who had been sold abroad. He also moved what a court is: the right of appeal to the popular assembly (ephesis) and the assembly of all citizens as final instance transferred judgement from a few officials to the governed. He refused to make himself tyrant and left the property classes in place; the settlement was a compromise that neither side accepted, the factional fighting continued, and within a generation Pisistratus established a tyranny. The measure survives not as an institution but as a precedent: that a political body may annul financial obligations it holds incompatible with the standing of its members.

**c. 500 BCE — Athenian demes, Council of 500, and sortition (Attica).** The demes were the smallest units of the reorganisation attributed to Cleisthenes: some 139 local bodies holding their own registers of citizens, their own cults, their own property and their own assemblies, into which membership passed by birth and registration rather than by wealth. Above them sat the Council of 500, fifty men from each tribe chosen by lot for a year, no one serving twice, preparing the agenda the assembly voted on; above that the assembly of all citizens, and around it the jury courts of several hundred citizens drawn by lot, in which there was no judge and the verdict was the panel's. Payment for jury service and for council duty made the offices reachable by the poor. The system was not overthrown from within but from outside: the Macedonian settlement of 322 BCE imposed a property qualification and the democracy of the lot was dismantled by an occupying power, restored and dismantled again under Roman pressure.

**c. 250 BCE — Ashoka's edicts and the dhamma administration (Maurya empire, South Asia).** After the Kalinga war Ashoka had ordinances of tolerance, non-violence and welfare cut into rock and pillar across the empire and appointed officials (the dhamma-mahamattas) to carry them out, including provisions for travellers, for prisoners, and for the protection of forests and animals. It is the earliest case on a large scale of a ruler committing himself in writing to limits on his own action and of a welfare mandate written into public law instead of left in custom. The edicts were not a charter held by subjects and gave them no body to enforce them; enforcement ran through the ruler's own officials. The structure did not outlive the dynasty — the empire broke up after Ashoka's death, and later rulers set up their own inscriptions without these obligations. What remains is the text in the rock, which is why the limits are still readable and the institution is not.

**c. 200 BCE–200 CE — Roman collegia and res communis omnium (Italy and the provinces).** Voluntary associations of craftsmen, traders, burial societies and neighbourhood cults, each with its own chest, its own rules (lex collegii), its own officers and its own meeting place, and each capable of holding property in common and of acting as a body before a magistrate; alongside them the jurists' category of res communis omnium, the things that belong to no one and to everyone — air, flowing water, the sea and its shore — which no private title could exhaust. The collegia worked, and because they worked they were watched: from the late Republic on they were licensed, restricted and periodically dissolved, and under the Empire they were increasingly folded into the state's own administration of the grain supply, the fire brigades and the funeral services. The category of the common thing survived in law precisely because it belonged to nobody and so threatened no one; the associations that had actually pooled resources did not survive as self-governing bodies.

**c. 30–100 CE — Early Christian congregations and the common purse (Eastern Mediterranean).** The earliest congregations pooled resources: members sold property and brought the proceeds to the assembly, the community maintained widows, orphans and prisoners, and deacons were appointed after a dispute over fairness in the daily distribution. Decisions were taken by the assembled membership, which chose its own officers, and the Pauline letters show congregations correcting one another and settling disputes internally instead of before secular courts. The practice was voluntary and local, with no jurisdiction beyond the members and no means of coercion; it was carried by shared belief, not by enforceable rules. As the movement grew and became the religion of the empire, the common purse became diocesan property administered by a hierarchy, and the assembly's power to appoint and to correct its officers narrowed to a clergy. The communal form did not fail; it was absorbed by the institution outside which it had begun.

**Early Middle Ages — Germanic Markgenossenschaft (central and western Europe).** The mark was the commons of a settlement or a group of settlements: woodland, pasture, water and waste held jointly, with use rights allocated by the mark association, and disputes settled in the mark court, which met under the open sky, was chaired by the mark judge, and decided by the assembled markgenossen — the same people who held the rights. Tithes, forest and hunting rights of a lord, and the growing claims of territorial princes pressed on the mark from outside, and inside the association rights were increasingly attached to particular farms rather than to the community of users. The mark court was absorbed into the seigneurial and later the territorial court, and the mark itself was dissolved in the enclosure and separation proceedings of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, in which the rights were converted into private title to be bought and sold.

**c. 500–530 — Mazdak and the Sasanian communal reform (Iran).** Under the Sasanian king Kavad I the preacher Mazdak advocated a programme of shared property, common granaries and relief for the poor, and for a time the court supported it: granaries were opened, goods were redistributed and the claim was made that surplus belongs to those who need it. It is the earliest attested attempt to put a doctrine of common property into state practice at imperial scale, and it was organised from above through the king's administration, not by assemblies of the users. Support was withdrawn as quickly as it had been given — the nobility and the Zoroastrian priesthood came out against it, Kavad's son Khosrow I reversed the policy, and after 528 the movement and its adherents were killed. Nothing of the organisation survived; the episode is known mainly from the hostile accounts of those who destroyed it.

**c. 900–1100 — Monastic confederations: Cluny, later Cîteaux (Burgundy).** Cluny built a federation rather than a collection of houses: the abbot of Cluny visited the priories, appointed their superiors, and the whole body of monks of the order were counted as monks of one monastery, with the customary, the liturgy and the estates administered in common. Cîteaux went further towards a constitution, with a general chapter of all abbots meeting annually, visitation by the abbot of the mother house, and granges worked by lay brothers so that the choir monks stayed free of the estates. The Cistercian general chapter is one of the earliest working representative assemblies in Europe with real power over its members. Both federations ended up as landlords: the exemption from tithes and the donated estates made them rich, the lay brothers' labour system collapsed in the fourteenth century, commendatory abbots appointed from outside took the revenues, and the houses were dissolved — in England in the 1530s by act of the crown, in France in 1790 by the revolution.

**c. 1170–1230 — Cathars and Waldensians (Languedoc, Lombardy, the Rhineland).** Two movements of voluntary poverty organised outside the church hierarchy: the Waldensians, founded by a merchant who gave away his goods, sent out wandering preachers in pairs and let laypeople, women included, preach without episcopal licence; the Cathars built a parallel church of perfected believers and ordinary adherents, supported by the gifts of the latter and tolerated for decades by local lords who protected them against the bishop. Both were assemblies of the convinced, not jurisdictions, and neither held a court. The church treated their autonomy as the offence: the Waldensians were excommunicated in 1184, the Cathars were attacked from 1209 with a crusade, followed by the Inquisition established in 1233, with the last stronghold at Montségur in 1244. The movement was destroyed by an outside power that had an army and courts, and what survived of the Waldensians survived by withdrawal into alpine valleys.

**1215 — Magna Carta (Runnymede, England).** A settlement forced on King John by rebel barons, sealed in June 1215 and reissued in altered form in 1216, 1217 and 1225. Its clauses bound the crown in writing for the first time: no taxation without common counsel, no free man imprisoned or deprived of his property except by lawful judgement of his peers or the law of the land, and — in the 1215 text — the restoration of the commons of wood, warren and fishery that had been enclosed by royal licence. Enforcement was the weak point from the start: the security clause gave twenty-five barons the right to distrain the king, and the charter was annulled by the pope within months; it held only because successive kings found it cheaper to confirm it than to fight it. Its clauses were narrowed over centuries, most of the substantive ones repealed in the nineteenth and twentieth, and what remains in the Statute Book is a remnant. It is the case that shows a written limit on ruler power can outlive the force that extracted it — and that the limit reaches only as far as the body willing to enforce it.

**1217 — Charter of the Forest (England).** Issued as a companion to Magna Carta, and for two centuries read alongside it, the Forest Charter dealt with the land the crown had claimed as hunting ground and withdrawn from ordinary law: it disafforested the woods and riverbanks taken into the royal forest since the reign of Henry II, restored the common rights of pannage, estovers, grazing and turbary to those who had held them, and set the forest courts under fixed procedure and fixed penalties, with the sworn men of the county present. It was confirmed more often than the charter it came with, and the forest eyres that enforced it were hated enough that their suspension was a standing demand of the commons. Its clauses were eroded by later perambulations and by the crown's reassertion of forest in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and most of what remained was repealed in 1858 and finally in 1971. The rights it protected did not survive the enclosure of the following centuries, but the practice of the sworn local jury survived inside it.

**13th–14th century — Swiss Eidgenossenschaft: Uri, Schwyz, Unterwalden, with later members (alpine valleys).** Valleys that held their pastures, forests and alpine passes in common bound themselves to one another by sworn oath (Eid) rather than by submission to a lord, and defended the arrangement militarily against the Habsburg claim to jurisdiction over them. Organisation ran through the valley commune: the assembly of the men able to bear arms, the elected officials, the common property of alp, forest and water, and for the confederation the Tagsatzung, an assembly of envoys carrying instructions from their communes and bound by them. The confederation was not destroyed; it was transformed. The Tagsatzung became a congress of sovereign cantons after 1648, the confederate form narrowed to the cantonal governments in 1848, and inside the cantons the commons were divided and privatised in the same nineteenth-century separation proceedings that dissolved the marks elsewhere.

**c. 1450–1600 — Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy (today's New York, Ontario and Quebec).** Five nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, later Tuscarora — brought together under the Great Law of Peace, with a Grand Council of fifty hereditary chiefs (royaneh) nominated by the clan mothers and recallable by them, decisions requiring consensus of the council, and the council holding no power to coerce a member nation: matters that concerned one nation were left to it. Land was held by the clan and the confederacy, not by individuals, and the wampum records carried the law without a written text. It did not fall; it was reduced. Treaties were made by the settler states with individual nations rather than with the confederacy, the land base was alienated piece by piece, the Indian Act administration imposed elected band councils in place of the traditional selection, and the confederacy survives as a ceremonial and legal authority whose decisions the surrounding states do not enforce.

**Medieval–18th century — Open-field system and manorial common rights (England, northern Europe).** Arable held in scattered strips within large open fields, farmed on a rotation agreed by the village, with the fields thrown open for grazing after harvest and the pasture, meadow, wood and waste held in common by the tenants of the manor, their use regulated by the manorial court through the jury of the tenants themselves — the same body that recorded surrenders of land, admitted new tenants and levied fines for overstocking the common. The court was the tenants' own instrument and the lord's at once, which is why the system could be administered at all and why it could be destroyed by a statute that bypassed it. The commons were ended by enclosure: by agreement among the larger holders, by private act of parliament, and increasingly by general enclosure acts, with the rights extinguished and converted into allotments of land or into nothing.

**1525 — Thomas Müntzer and the German Peasants' War (Thuringia, Swabia, Franconia).** The peasant leagues of 1524–25 set down their demands in written articles — the Twelve Articles of Memmingen, the most printed document of the Reformation — and claimed the right of the congregation to choose its own pastor and to test the scripture, the reduction of tithes to what the gospel justified, and the return of common woods, waters and pastures that had passed into seigneurial property. They organised through elected delegates of the village bands and through the articles themselves, which circulated and were sworn to. Müntzer, preaching at Allstedt and Mühlhausen, pressed the claim further: that all things should be held in common and that the godless lords had forfeited their rule. The leagues were broken within weeks: the princes' armies destroyed the main body at Frankenhausen in May 1525, Müntzer was executed, the articles never conceded. The defeat was military and final, and it took the commons question out of German politics for centuries.

**1649 — The Diggers, Gerrard Winstanley, St George's Hill (Surrey, England).** In April 1649 a group of some twenty began to dig and sow the waste land on St George's Hill, declaring the earth a common treasury and asserting that the common land belonged to those who worked it, not to the lord of the manor or to the parish. They organised as a community of labour with communal stores and no hired hands, published their case in pamphlets that were read across the country, and planted further colonies at Cobham, Wellingborough and elsewhere. They held no court and had no title in law, which was the whole of their difficulty: the local lords brought actions, the Council of State ordered them removed, the army dispersed the colonies in 1650, and the harvests were trodden down by soldiers and by hired men. Winstanley's later programme in *The Law of Freedom* (1652) was written after the attempt had failed and knew it.

**1750–1850 — The Enclosure Acts (England and Wales).** Several thousand private and general acts of parliament reorganised the open fields and commons of a large part of England: commissioners were appointed by statute, surveyed the parish, extinguished the common rights and allotted the land to those who could prove a title, hedged and fenced it, and registered the result. It worked as designed, and that is the entry: the largest redistribution of land in English history was carried out legally, by a body in which the commoners had no vote, using a procedure that required no consent from those whose rights it abolished. Resistance was real and was treated as riot: the Diggers' successors, the Swing disturbances of 1830, the burnings of hedges and the attacks on commissioners were put down by troops and prosecuted, hundreds transported. The smallholders and cottagers who could not prove a title received nothing; by 1876 some 1.5 per cent of the population owned most of the land.

**1800–1825 — Robert Owen and New Lanark (Lanarkshire, Scotland).** Owen took over the cotton mills at New Lanark in 1800 and ran them as a test of whether an industrial works can be organised around the welfare of its workers: infant schools and a school for children from the age of two, a shop at near cost, sick and old-age provision paid out of profits, and no corporal punishment. The mills stayed profitable, which was the point — the experiment was offered as proof that humane organisation is not a subsidy. But Owen owned the works; the order rested on an owner's decision, not on a rule the workers held, and they had no share in its direction. When he tried to extend the principle to a co-operative community at New Harmony in Indiana from 1825, the community dissolved within a few years amid internal dispute and the absence of any binding constitution. New Lanark shows that the model can work; it also shows that a model depending on the favour of an owner is not a commons.

**1871 — The Paris Commune (Paris).** After the siege and the collapse of the government of national defence, the Paris sections refused to disarm and elected a council of eighty-odd members in March 1871; the council organised into commissions, worked through the arrondissement committees and the ward assemblies, and operated on mandates that were short, published and revocable, with salaries fixed at a worker's wage. Within two months it had decreed the remission of rents, the separation of church and state, the reopening of workshops as co-operatives and the return of abandoned factories to associations of workers. It had no time to build an administration and never controlled the country. It was destroyed by the Versailles government with the Prussian army standing by: the federal troops entered in the last week of May, some ten to twenty thousand were killed in the week that followed, and thousands more were executed, imprisoned or deported.

**1918–19 — Council movements in Germany, Austria and Hungary.** Workers' and soldiers' councils appeared across the Central Powers in the last weeks of the war, elected in the workshops and the barracks, and in the first months of 1919 they held real authority in Berlin, Vienna, Budapest and the industrial districts: they controlled food, transport and demobilisation, and the Austrian and German governments existed alongside them. Organisation was by delegate from workshop and unit, mandating and recallable, with the congress of councils as the claim to sovereignty. They were not primarily destroyed by the old order but pre-empted by the new governments: the German congress of councils in December 1918 voted against council sovereignty and for the national assembly, the socialist leadership allied with the army command and used the Freikorps against the Berlin and Bavarian councils, and in Hungary the council republic collapsed under the Romanian invasion and the defection of its own social democrats. The councils were also divided over supply, and their rivalry for food and coal did much of the work for their opponents.

**1936–39 — Spanish collectives and the anarchist councils, CNT-FAI (Catalonia, Aragon, Valencia).** In the weeks after the military rising of July 1936 the CNT took over much of Catalan and Aragonese industry and agriculture: some four hundred agrarian collectives, the tramways, the ports, the telephone system and a large part of the textile industry, run by committees elected in the workplaces and the villages, with the union and the collective overlapping. In Aragon the collectives organised a regional federation with its own council, its own supply and its own schools. They were destroyed from two directions. The Republican state, with the communists in the ministries and Soviet arms behind them, dismantled the collectives by decree from 1937 — the agrarian collectives were dissolved, the council of Aragon was broken up by armoured units in August 1937 — and Franco's victory in 1939 ended the rest, with expropriations, executions and prison.

## 1945–1991 · Yugoslavia (SFRY)
**Context:** Break with Stalin (1948) left Yugoslavia outside the Soviet bloc and in need of legitimacy that was neither Soviet nor liberal. The answer: a federation of six republics and two autonomous provinces, a collective presidency rotating annually, and a founding role in the Non-Aligned Movement (1961).
**Approach:** *Social ownership* instead of state or private property; enterprises run by elected workers' councils from 1950, formalised as the organising principle of society in the 1963 constitution. Councils at enterprise, commune, and republic level, delegates bound to their base rather than free representatives. The centre temporary by construction.
**Status:** Undermined by the League of Communists, which sat outside the self-management structure and decided what the structure was allowed to decide. Half-introduced market reforms (1965) reversed, leaving enterprises neither planned nor competitive; no mechanism for resolving economic divergence between republics. Ended in debt, hyperinflation, and war in 1991.
**Learning:** Self-management is not a property of institutions but of competence: councils that cannot decide the questions that matter are decoration. R38ET takes the council cascade and the deliberately temporary centre, and avoids both gaps — a norm hierarchy that settles conflicts between levels, and a mechanism that keeps any actor *inside* the structure rather than above it.

**1960s– — Ecovillages, with the Global Ecovillage Network, GEN Europe from 1995 (international).** Intentional communities organised around shared land, common buildings and a low material throughput, from the communes of the late 1960s through Findhorn, Solheimar, Auroville and hundreds of smaller settlements, federated from 1991 in the Global Ecovillage Network and from 1995 in GEN Europe, which gave the settlements an office, a shared set of standards and a way of talking to funders. Organisation is by consensus or by council of the founding members, with land held by a trust or a company and use rights granted to residents. Few of them were destroyed; most thinned out. The internal causes recur: the founding generation's authority was never converted into a rule, the resource base was over-estimated, membership turnover exceeded the capacity to socialise new members, and legal title to the land stayed with whoever had raised the money.

**1971–73 — Project Cybersyn, Stafford Beer (Santiago, Chile).** Under the Allende government Beer designed a real-time coordination system for the economy: telex links from state enterprises to an operations room in Santiago, a statistical model (Cyberstride) that reported deviations in production, and a cybernetic principle — the viable system model — meant to give each level of the economy the autonomy it needed while keeping the whole coherent. It was built and used: during the lorry owners' strike in October 1972 the government coordinated distribution over the telex network, and the system was designed to shorten the feedback between shop floor and decision rather than to centralise command. It ended with the coup of 11 September 1973; the operations room was destroyed and the project with it. Its unresolved problem is the one that counts here: the design presupposed a state that would not misuse the information it collected, and the state that replaced it did.

**2006– — Transition Towns, beginning in Totnes (international).** Beginning with the initiative in Totnes in 2006, towns and neighbourhoods formed groups to plan their own reduction in fossil-fuel dependence: an Energy Descent Action Plan written locally, working groups on food, transport, energy and local money, and a published twelve-step procedure that anyone could take up without permission or licence. The form spread to hundreds of initiatives in Britain and abroad within a few years, with a national hub and a small coordinating staff. Nothing destroyed it. It thinned out: the groups depended on a few volunteers, the plan documents were rarely acted on by the councils they were addressed to, the network's central office could not fund itself at the scale of its membership, and the initiative form proved easier to start than to hand on.

**2012– — Rojava: Democratic Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria.** In the vacuum left by the Syrian army's withdrawal in 2012 the PYD and its allies built an administration in the Kurdish, Arab, Assyrian and Yazidi areas of the north: communes of a few hundred households as the base unit, people's councils above them with co-presidencies and a fixed gender share of every office, cantonal assemblies, and a charter of 2014 declaring the cantons' autonomy and the rights of the constituent communities. Decisions on local matters run through the communes, those on the cantons through the councils, and the armed defence is separate and has been the decisive actor. It has not been destroyed and it is not secure: it has been attacked by Turkey in successive campaigns since 2016, held under blockade by neighbouring authorities, and its economy and administration depend heavily on outside support and on the proceeds of oil and of the war.

**2019– — DEAL networks, Doughnut Economics Action Lab (international).** Set up to turn the framework of planetary and social boundaries into municipal practice, the lab works with cities and with a network of local governments: Amsterdam, Copenhagen, Berlin, Brussels and dozens more have adopted the framework, published city portraits of their own consumption against the boundaries, and reorganised departments, procurement and budget reporting around them. Organisation is a professional office and a voluntary city network, with no jurisdiction over any of its members and no power to bind them. That is also the open problem: the member cities keep their own budgets and their own electorates, the framework sets targets without instruments, and where a city has changed its government the programme has been renamed, reduced or dropped.

## What the register shows

Read straight through, the entries divide along one line more sharply than along any century.

Institutions that held jurisdiction — the manorial court, the mark court, the Magna Carta and the forest charter, the Commune, the city council — could bind a member against his will, levy a fine, keep a record and enforce it. Almost every one of them began as the instrument of the members and ended as the instrument of someone else: the court that had once recorded a common right later extinguished it, and the records it kept are the evidence of both.

Institutions that had coordination capacity — the monastic chapter, the confederate Tagsatzung, the workers' councils, Cybersyn, the networks — could align decisions without coercing anyone, and they are the ones that fall fastest: they hold while the commitment behind them holds, and when it lapses there is nothing underneath to catch them.

Where the register records destruction, the destroyer is external, named and equipped: the Macedonian settlement at Athens, the papacy and the French crown against the Cathars, the princes at Frankenhausen, the Council of State against the Diggers, the Versailles army in the Bloody Week, the Freikorps, the Republican ministries in Aragon, the coup of 1973. Where internal causes dominate — Mesopotamian salinisation, New Harmony's lack of a binding constitution, ecovillage resource exhaustion, the council movements' race for supply — the end arrives without an enemy, which is why those entries are harder to use as warnings: there is nobody to blame and no line to draw.

Three entries leave a text that outlives the body that wrote it: Ashoka's edicts, the Twelve Articles, the Forest Charter. In each case the limits are still legible and the institution is not, because a text can be copied by anyone while a body of users has to be maintained. The register's most quoted documents are its least successful attempts, and that is a property of documents, not of the attempts.

The entries also distinguish between models that worked and models that were commons. New Lanark was profitable and depended on an owner's decision; New Harmony was a commons and had no rule to hold it. The confederations that lasted — the mark, the Haudenosaunee, the Swiss valleys — combined the two: a rule the members held and a court or council with the standing to apply it.
